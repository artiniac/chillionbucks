👨‍👩‍👦 For grown-ups

What this site is, and what it is not.

Free. No ads. No accounts.

Nothing to sign up for, nothing to buy, nothing collected. The only thing saved on your device is the sound on/off setting.

Where the videos come from

Every video is a real YouTube video from a teacher or a kids’ channel, hand-picked and embedded with YouTube’s privacy-enhanced player. If one goes stale, it gets swapped.

How to use it with your kid

  • Play Need or Want together and argue about pizza.
  • Pick a real goal, then use Piggy Bank Power to show how taps add up.
  • Slide the Chill-o-Meter and let them see the “baby money” number jump.

The Builder and photos

The Builder can use a photo of your child’s room as the background. The photo is resized and kept only in this browser on this device (it never leaves the phone or computer, and there is no server to send it to). “New world” or clearing the browser’s site data removes it. Finished worlds are saved as small pictures on the same device only.

How the money works: blocks, tiles, and building parts in the Builder are free. Pools, rides, cars, and characters cost bucks. Bucks come from work: the Job Board has quick jobs (clean the pool, wash a car, paint a fence, deliver packages, feed the animals, run the lemonade stand) that pay a bill, and build orders that pay a bill when a build is complete. Every bill has to be dragged into the piggy before it can be spent. Bigger bills only come from more work: a $5 bill for most jobs, a $20 every fifth job, a $100 every twentieth, and the fat stack and pot of gold are for the truly dedicated (100 and 250 jobs).

The savings goals are real things. Edit goals.js to swap in the exact items you would actually buy: a picture, the real price, and the store link. The store button says “grown-up” on purpose and opens in a new tab; nothing on this site takes money or collects anything.

Why “Chillion”?

Leo (almost 5) invented the word. Dad explained that a chillion bucks is when you can chill all day and still have a lot of money. Turns out that is a pretty good definition of financial freedom.
